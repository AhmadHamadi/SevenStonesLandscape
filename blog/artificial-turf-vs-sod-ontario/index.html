Artificial Turf vs Sod in Ontario: An Honest Cost Comparison

By Riaad, Owner & Project Manager · ICPI Certified Installer · Last updated September 1, 2026

September 1, 2026|By Seven Stones Landscape

Artificial turf keeps coming up on Southern Ontario quotes, and the numbers homeowners are shown are rarely the whole picture. We install both turf and natural sod, so we genuinely do not mind which you choose — and that is exactly why the maths below is shown the way it is, including the fork most turf marketing skips. Below: what each actually costs in the GTA, the ten-year total done both ways, where turf genuinely wins, where it loses, and why the base underneath decides everything. If you are weighing up a new lawn, our sod installation and grading and drainage pages cover how we prepare a lot.

The short answer

Artificial turf costs roughly $15 to $25 per square foot installed in the GTA. Sod costs $4 to $7 per square foot the way we install it, with grading and 4 to 6 inches of screened topsoil underneath. So turf starts about three to five times more expensive, lasts 15 to 20 years, and then has to be replaced in full.

Whether that is worth it comes down to one question that most turf marketing avoids: do you pay someone to maintain your lawn? If you do, turf tends to pull ahead somewhere around year seven to ten. If you mow your own grass, sod stays cheaper for the entire life of the turf. Everything else in this article is detail underneath that one fork.

What artificial turf costs in Ontario

Professionally installed artificial turf in the Greater Toronto Area runs about $15 to $25 per square foot. On a 1,000 square foot front lawn that is $15,000 to $25,000. The range is wide because two very different jobs get sold under the same name.

At the top of the range you are paying for the lawn to be excavated, an aggregate base placed and compacted in lifts, a drainage layer built to move water off clay, edge restraint so the turf cannot creep, seams glued rather than stapled, and infill brushed in properly. At the bottom of the range someone is rolling turf over a thin scattering of gravel. Both look identical the week they are finished. Only one of them is still flat after three Ontario winters.

Add-ons that are often quoted separately: drainage, infill material, weed barrier, and disposal of the old lawn. When you compare two turf quotes, compare the base depth and the drainage plan, not the turf product. The fibres are rarely what fails.

What sod costs, and why quotes vary so much

You will see sod quoted anywhere from $2 to $7 per square foot around the GTA, and the gap confuses people. It is not the grass. Sod is close to a commodity, and the roll a budget installer lays is usually similar to ours.

The difference is what happens before the roll goes down. At $2 to $4 per square foot, sod is generally being laid on whatever is already there. At the $4 to $7 we quote, the old lawn is stripped and hauled away, the lot is regraded so water runs away from the foundation, and 4 to 6 inches of screened topsoil goes in so roots have something to drive into. On the clay that runs through Hamilton, Halton and Peel, that prep is the entire difference between a lawn that establishes and a lawn that browns out in its first August.

It is the same principle as a paver base. The part you cannot see afterwards is the part that decides how long it lasts. We wrote about the same trade-off in sod versus seed, and about timing in the best time to install sod in Ontario.

The ten-year maths, done honestly

Here is a 1,000 square foot lawn over ten years, using GTA figures. The maintenance line is where the whole comparison turns, so it is shown both ways.

Installed cost$15,000 – $25,000$4,000 – $7,000$4,000 – $7,000
Maintenance over 10 yearsRinsing, brushing, infill top-ups$15,000 – $25,000Your time, plus fuel, seed and fertilizer

Read the middle column and turf looks like an easy win. Read the right-hand column and it does not. Both are true; they are just describing different households. Before you accept anyone's ten-year comparison, check which of those two columns they quietly assumed.

One more figure that belongs in the calculation: at year 15 to 20 the turf is replaced, and you pay the full installed cost again. Sod at year 15 is simply an older lawn.

Where turf genuinely wins

We install both, so we have no reason to talk you out of either. These are the yards where we would put turf in ourselves.

  • Deep shade where grass has already failed twice. Under a mature maple canopy, past a certain point, no blend of fescue is going to hold. If you have already re-sodded and watched it thin out again, that is real evidence.
  • Dog runs. Turf takes traffic and digging that grass cannot, and it ends the mud problem permanently.
  • Small, awkward spaces. A narrow side yard or a townhouse strip where getting a mower in is a chore, and where the total square footage keeps the price sane.
  • Rooftop terraces and anywhere with no soil. No contest.
  • You are already paying a lawn company. This is the honest financial case, and it is a real one.

Where turf genuinely loses

  • Full-sun yards used by children. Synthetic fibres hold heat that living grass sheds through evaporation. In July, a south-facing turf lawn gets uncomfortable in a way grass does not.
  • Large open lawns. The economics stop working as the area grows. At 3,000 square feet, turf is a $45,000 to $75,000 decision.
  • Clay lots with existing drainage problems. Turf does not fix drainage; it hides it. Water that used to sit on a lawn will sit under turf instead, and freeze there. Fix the grading first, whatever surface goes on top. That is what our yard grading and drainage work is for.
  • If you value the living part of a lawn. Turf does not cool the air around it, does not absorb rainfall, and supports nothing. That matters to some homeowners a great deal and to others not at all, and it is a fair thing to weigh.

The base under turf is a hardscape base

This is the part homeowners are most often not told, and it is the part we care about most, because it is the same work we do under every patio and driveway we build.

Artificial turf is not a surface you lay on soil. The lawn has to be excavated, an aggregate base placed and compacted in lifts, and the whole thing graded to a positive fall so water leaves. The turf backing is perforated, so water goes straight through the surface. Everything then depends on where it goes next.

On the clay through Hamilton, Halton and Peel, water that reaches an under-built base has nowhere to go. It sits, it freezes, and it lifts the surface. That is Ontario freeze-thaw doing to a turf base exactly what it does to a poorly built patio base, and we have written about the same mechanism in why Ontario concrete cracks. A turf installation quoted without an excavation depth and a drainage plan is being quoted by someone who has not thought about the second winter.

This is the part of a turf job we care most about, because it is the same excavation, compaction and drainage work we do under every patio and driveway we build. When we install turf, the base goes in to hardscape standard. When you are comparing quotes from anyone else, ask for the excavation depth and the drainage plan in writing.

If your property sits inside a conservation authority regulated area, that excavation can also need approval before it starts, because placing and removing fill and altering grade are regulated activities. That catches people out across the Golden Horseshoe, and it is worth checking by address before anyone books a machine.

What we would tell you on site

We install both surfaces, which means we have no stake in your answer. That is worth saying plainly, because most articles on this question are written by a company that only sells one of the two.

Most yards we walk should stay natural grass, because most of the lawns that failed did not fail on the grass. They failed on compacted subsoil, a lot that drains toward the house instead of away, or a shade blend that was never specified. Fix those three things and a sod lawn performs at a quarter of the installed cost of turf, and we would rather tell you that than sell you the expensive option.

The exceptions are real though, and when we see one we say so. Deep shade that has already beaten two lawns, a dedicated dog run, a tight side yard nobody wants to drag a mower into, or a household already writing cheques to a lawn maintenance company. In those yards turf is the better buy and we will install it.

What we would not do is decide before seeing the yard. Book a visit and we will dig a test hole, look at how the water moves, check the light, and give you a straight answer for your lot. It is free and there is nothing attached to it.

Artificial Turf vs Sod: Common Questions

Professionally installed artificial turf runs roughly $15 to $25 per square foot in the GTA. That is three to five times the installed cost of sod. The spread comes from the base: turf laid on a properly excavated, compacted and drained base sits at the top of that range, and turf rolled over thin gravel sits at the bottom. The cheap version is the one that ripples and holds water.
Good quality turf, installed correctly, lasts about 15 to 20 years before the fibres flatten and the colour goes. That is the number to use in any cost comparison. Turf installed on an inadequate base fails much sooner, and it fails by moving rather than wearing out, which is not something you can fix without lifting it.
It depends entirely on whether you pay someone to maintain your lawn. If you contract out mowing, fertilizing and weed control at $1,500 to $2,500 a year in the GTA, turf usually comes out ahead somewhere between year seven and year ten. If you mow your own lawn, that maintenance line mostly disappears and sod stays cheaper for the full life of the turf. Most turf marketing quietly assumes you are paying a lawn company.
Yes, and this is the drawback that gets glossed over. Synthetic fibres retain far more heat than living grass, which cools itself through evaporation. On a full-sun south-facing yard in July it can become genuinely uncomfortable underfoot. Lighter infills, shade from trees or a sail, and a rinse before use all help, but they do not make it behave like grass.
It drains only as well as the base under it. Turf backing is perforated, so water passes through the surface easily, but it then has to get away through the aggregate below. On the clay that covers most of the Golden Horseshoe, water that reaches an under-built base sits there, freezes, and lifts the surface. Freeze-thaw is the reason a turf base in Ontario has to be built to the same standard as a patio base, not to the standard used in a dry climate.
It should not be. The old lawn has to come out, along with enough soil to make room for a compacted aggregate base. Turf laid straight over grass will settle unevenly as the organic material underneath breaks down, and it will hold water. If a quote does not include excavation and a base depth, it is not quoting the job properly.
It handles traffic and digging far better than grass, which is the main reason dog owners choose it, and there is no mud. The trade-off is that urine does not break down the way it does in soil, so turf in a dog run needs rinsing and a permeable infill or it will hold odour, particularly in summer heat.
Yes. We install artificial turf and natural sod, and we build the excavated, compacted and drained bases that sit under both. That is deliberately why this comparison is not a sales pitch for one of them: we are paid either way, so the only thing we care about is that you pick the surface that suits your yard and how you actually live in it. Book a visit and we will dig a test hole, look at how the water moves, and tell you which way we would go on your specific lot.
